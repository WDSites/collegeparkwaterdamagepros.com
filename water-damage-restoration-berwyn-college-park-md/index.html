Which Berwyn This Is

The name is shared. This page is about the Berwyn neighborhood inside the City of College Park, Maryland. It is not the separate town of Berwyn Heights, and it has nothing to do with places named Berwyn in other states.

The City of College Park lists a Berwyn District Civic Association and a Berwyn neighborhood watch on its neighborhoods page. The city's council district description groups Berwyn with the part of Lakeland west of the CSX tracks, east of US 1 and south of Greenbelt Road, with the southern edge along Paint Branch. We use those landmarks rather than a street boundary, because we have not found an official one.

Homes, Owners and a Small Commercial Strip

The University of Maryland's off-campus housing office describes Berwyn as one of College Park's oldest and most historic neighborhoods, with nearly 400 homes in mixed architectural styles and about 69 percent owner-occupied. It also notes a small commercial area with shops along Berwyn Road. Local press says the area was developed beginning about 1885 beside the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad.

Those figures tell us who is likely to call. Most callers will be owners who deal with their own insurer and decide on repairs themselves. The rest are tenants, who should tell the landlord about a leak at once and take photos. Shop owners along Berwyn Road face a different problem, because a wet floor can close a business. Where Household Water Damage Usually Starts

These are general sources of water in any house, not claims about Berwyn. Each one has its own cleanup page.

  • A split or leaking supply line behind a wall or under a floor. See burst pipe water damage.
  • A washing machine hose, dishwasher line or ice maker connection. See appliance leak water damage.
  • A roof or flashing leak that stains a ceiling. See roof leak water damage.
  • Rain that finds a way in at a basement window or foundation wall.

First-Hour Safety Steps

Safety comes before cleanup. Water and electricity do not mix, and sewage is a hazard on its own.

Stay out of risky water

Keep people and pets out of water that may be contaminated or that touches outlets, cords or the panel. Do not touch breakers or electrical equipment while standing in water.

Stop the source

Close the main water shutoff only when you can reach it safely. Shut off a leaking appliance supply valve the same way.

Call for immediate danger

Call 911 for fire, shock or structural danger. For sewage, call the utility, WSSC Water, before you clean.

Record it from a safe spot

Take photos and short videos of the water, the source and the damage before anything is moved.

Protect what is dry

Lift small items and papers off wet floors if it is safe. Leave heavy furniture alone.

How Extraction and Drying Work

A restoration crew starts by finding the source and checking for hazards. Standing water is removed with pumps and extractors. Moisture meters and a thermal camera show how far the water traveled into floors, walls and ceilings, which is often further than it looks.

Air movers and dehumidifiers then dry the structure on a plan, and readings are taken until materials reach their dry standard. The drying steps follow what ANSI/IICRC S500, an industry reference, describes for water damage work. Household fans and a shop vacuum are not enough for sewage or other contaminated water, which calls for protective gear and removal of porous materials. Our water mitigation page explains the drying stage in detail.

Insurance Documentation

Photos, moisture readings and an itemized scope can help document a loss. Whether the policy pays depends on the cause of the damage and the policy terms, so read your policy and ask your insurer before you agree to anything. We do not decide coverage, and nothing here promises that a claim will be approved.

If the water came from a sudden plumbing failure, write down when you found it and what you shut off. If it came from rain or sewer backup, ask your insurer whether those causes are covered at all.
