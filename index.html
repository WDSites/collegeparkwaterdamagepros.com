Water Damage Restoration in College Park Starts With the Water Source

Water damage restoration is the work of finding the source, removing standing water, drying wet materials and rebuilding what cannot be saved. In College Park the source is rarely mysterious. It is usually an aging supply line, a failed appliance hose, a roof leak, or stormwater and sewer water pushing into a low basement.

The city sits where Paint Branch and Indian Creek meet to form the Northeast Branch of the Anacostia River, and the University of Maryland covers about 41 percent of the city's surface.

That mix of creek lowlands, older streets and busy rental housing shapes the calls a restoration company sees. Before we plan any drying, we ask where the water came from, because the answer decides what has to be removed and what can be saved.

Neighborhood age matters too. Old Town and Calvert Hills are among the city's oldest neighborhoods, while much of the infill after World War II was ranch and split-level housing. Older homes tend to carry older plumbing and plaster or lath walls, which dry differently from modern drywall. A crew that knows this arrives with the right extraction tools and the right questions.

What happened in Calvert Hills in September 2020

On September 10, 2020, a heavy rain event flooded parts of Calvert Hills. According to City Council minutes from January 11, 2021, a councilmember described stormwater flowing into houses and, in some cases, sewage backing up through toilets, drains and sinks. A WSSC Water representative told the council that stormwater flooding is not WSSC's responsibility and that backflow preventers at the property line were a short-term option.

If your basement takes on stormwater or sewage, call before you start cleaning. WSSC staff told the council that residents should call so the utility can investigate, and that photos and video of the damage are worth taking first. Then call us for extraction and drying.

Calvert Hills has a documented history of drainage and sewer backup problems, and Prince George's County has a storm drainage project there for that reason. The steps are the same everywhere: stop the source, extract, dry, document.

What to Do Before the Crew Arrives

The first hour matters. These steps are safe for most homeowners and keep the damage from growing while you wait.

Stop the source

Shut off the main water valve if the leak is from a supply line, or turn off the appliance that is leaking.

Mind the electricity

Turn off power to wet areas only if the panel is dry and you can reach it safely. If water touches outlets or the panel, stay out and call the utility or 911 first.

Protect what you can lift

Raise valuables, books, shoes and small furniture off wet floors. Pull rugs only if they are light.

Take photos and video

Record the water, the damage and any wet belongings before anything is moved. Your insurer will ask for this.

If sewage is involved, keep people and pets out of the water and do not try to clean it yourself. Contaminated water needs protective equipment and a proper plan.

Insurance Claims

Documentation That Helps Your Claim

A clear record for your insurer

We take photos, moisture readings and written notes of what was wet and what was removed, so you can hand your insurer a clean record. Coverage decisions belong to your insurer, so check your policy and ask your agent what applies.

General rules help you ask better questions. The Maryland Insurance Administration says homeowners policies generally do not cover flooding, which is covered by a separate flood policy. A sudden burst pipe is generally covered for the water damage it causes, though not for repairing the pipe itself.

Sewer and drain backup usually needs a separate endorsement. Maryland law requires insurers to offer that coverage in writing when a homeowners policy is applied for and when it renews, so look at your policy to see whether you said yes. Seepage through a foundation wall is treated as a maintenance issue and is generally not covered.
