Calvert Hills is a residential neighborhood inside the City of College Park, and the city lists the Calvert Hills Citizens Association among its neighborhood groups. The City also calls it a National Registry district. A housing profile from the University of Maryland's Office of Community Engagement counts 459 housing units, about three-quarters of them one-unit detached, with roughly 60 percent owner-occupied and 40 percent rented. That page is undated, so treat the figures as approximate.

That mix means calls here come from owners who live with the house every day and from tenants who may not know where the shutoff is. Both need the same first answer: what kind of water is it, and where did it come from?

Where Calvert Hills Sits and Why It Gets Named

We do not claim an official boundary for the neighborhood, because we found none. The County's drainage factsheet for the area outlines a project zone instead, running from the CSX and MARC tracks on the east to Baltimore Avenue on the west, and from Albion Road on the south to Calvert Road on the north.

The County notes the zone may reach west of Baltimore Avenue. Streets named in the project include Knox Road, Dartmouth Avenue and Calvert Road, and Calvert Park holds an underground stormwater vault.

A project zone is not a neighborhood line, so check your own address with the City's neighborhood lookup if it matters. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status has not been verified, so check the FEMA Flood Map Service Center for your own address.

What the Record Actually Shows

The Diamondback, the University of Maryland's student newspaper, reported that a 2009 storm flooded Calvert Hills and damaged property. It said the County later found the drainage system had never been built to the 10-year standard, announced a project in 2013 and spent nearly seven years on the design. That is press reporting, not a County finding we could read directly.

The same paper reported that on September 10, 2020, Knox Road flooded and one basement took roughly calf-deep water through sewage drains. A council member's survey of Calvert Hills and Old Town together counted 49 households with damage, 24 of them with sewage backflow. That count covers both neighborhoods, so it says nothing about Calvert Hills alone.

In February 2022, WTOP reported that the City planned a study of its 12 sub-watersheds using federal stimulus money, with Calvert Hills as the focus. We could not find published results of that study.

What the County does and does not claim

The County's own factsheet describes the Calvert Hills drainage project as not intended to be a flood solution. A project can reduce street and yard flooding without ending every basement problem.

The County and the City each publish project updates, and their completion dates do not match. Check the City's Calvert Hills drainage page for current status instead of relying on a date quoted elsewhere, including here.

Stormwater or Sewer: Who Handles What

The two kinds of water come through different pipes and different agencies. Prince George's County handles stormwater management in the city. WSSC Water handles the sanitary sewer and the drinking water supply. At a January 2021 City Council meeting, a WSSC speaker described the sanitary and storm systems as separate and said WSSC was studying the area with surcharge modeling, camera inspection and smoke testing.

The same minutes record WSSC asking residents to call before cleaning up and to document the damage with photos or video. They also record the claims practice as paying on depreciated value. WSSC's website adds that the owner maintains the sewer service from the property line to the house, so a clog on your side is yours to fix.

Safety First: The First Hour in a Calvert Hills Basement

Do not wade in

If water is touching outlets, appliances or a panel, stay out and keep children and pets out. Do not touch breakers or plug in equipment while standing in water.

Treat drain water as contaminated

Do not run a household vacuum or fans on sewage. Do not rinse it down another drain or try to scoop it by hand.

Shut off water only if safe

Close the main valve when the supply is the source and the valve is dry and reachable. For gas, a smell of gas or any fire risk, call 911.

Photograph from a safe spot

Take wide shots of the water line and close shots of damaged items. Note the time you first saw the water.

Extraction and Drying After a Storm or Backup

Standing water comes out first, using submersible pumps and truck-mounted or portable extractors. Then the crew measures what stayed wet with moisture meters and a thermal camera. Wet carpet, pad and the lower part of drywall are often cut out, because they hold water and cannot be dried reliably in place. Air movers and dehumidifiers then run until readings return to a dry standard. Flood damage restoration covers how contaminated water changes that plan.

Sewage is different. Porous materials that touched it are removed and hard surfaces are cleaned and sanitized under containment. Household fans do not make that safe, and drying alone is not enough. ANSI/IICRC S500 is the industry reference for how water categories are handled.

Documenting for Your Insurer and WSSC

Photos and video taken before anything is moved help describe the loss. Moisture readings and an itemized scope of removed and dried materials can support a restoration record. Coverage depends on the cause of the damage and your policy, and stormwater, sewer backup and a plumbing failure are often treated differently. Read your policy and ask your insurer what applies. Nothing in our paperwork promises payment.

If sewage was involved, tell WSSC and ask about its claims process. Keep receipts for anything you paid for, and keep damaged items until you know whether they need to be shown.
